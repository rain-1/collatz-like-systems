**Common facts (tier 1).** (i) The map is a bijection of the positive integers: each residue-class branch is an increasing affine bijection from its class onto one target class, and the target classes partition Z_{>0} (checked algebraically; spot-checked against an explicit inverse in the check script). (ii) For an injective map, fate (b) is impossible: if the orbit of x first meets a cycle C at y=T^k(x) with k>=1 and x not in C, then y has two distinct preimages (T^{k-1}(x) and its predecessor in C). So FATE is "returns" or "infinite". (iii) For a bijection, "every orbit eventually periodic" means "every orbit is a finite cycle".

**FATE (OPEN, tier 5).** This is literally Collatz's 1932 problem: is the orbit of 8 under T finite? (Lagarias 1985 survey, and his historical note on Collatz's notebooks.) Proved part: by (ii) the only possibilities are a return to 8 or an infinite orbit.
What was tried:
- Simulation: no return in 1e5 steps (orbit reaches ~8000 bits); baseline reports 2e4 steps. Evidence only.
- Monotone/drift: average log-multiplier (1/3)log(32/27) > 0 suggests divergence, but T has cycles and no monotone quantity exists along all orbits.
- Congruence separators: for every M <= 150 the forward closure of T(8) mod M in the residue graph contains 8 mod M, so no periodic first-step separator exists (checks/perm_1932_s8.py). Reference certificate_search_proofs.txt sec. 5 (unreviewed) gives an argument that this holds for every M.
- Base-3/base-2 automata: T reads its branch from the low ternary digit but multiplies by 2 or 4, so it does not act as a finite suffix rewrite in base 2 or base 3; I found no automaton or suffix/pushdown presentation of the kind used elsewhere in the census.
Nothing tried gives a proof; the problem is the named open one.

**GLOBAL (OPEN, tier 5).** "Yes" would settle FATE as "returns"; "no" needs one provably infinite orbit, not known for T.

## Exact-time connections between residue classes

**Proposition.** Write $m=2^e3^s u$ with $\gcd(u,6)=1$. For any two residues $a,b$ modulo $m$ and **every** $k\ge s+e+u-1$, there are infinitely many positive integers $x$ such that
$$
x\equiv a\pmod m,\qquad T^k(x)\equiv b\pmod m.
$$
The iterates here are iterates of a single integer, even though the residue graph allows different representatives on successive edges.

**Proof.** A word of $k$ prescribed input residues modulo 3 specifies exactly one starting class modulo $3^k$. Indeed, after $j$ prescribed steps, the iterate has slope $Q/3^j$ with $Q$ a power of 2. Increasing a compatible start by $3^j$ increases that iterate by $Q$. Exactly one of the three lifts of the start modulo $3^{j+1}$ therefore gives the next prescribed residue. This proves the assertion by induction, including the empty word.

Choose the first $s$ letters by iterating any positive representative of $a$. For the last $e$ letters, take a positive representative of $b$, follow its unique inverse orbit for $e$ steps, and read those branches forward. Every realization of this final word ends at $b$ modulo $2^e$. Indeed its affine slope has numerator divisible by $2^e$ and odd denominator, so any two outputs of that word agree modulo $2^e$.

Modulo $u$, each branch is a permutation. After the chosen prefix the residue is fixed by $a$, and the suffix has a unique input residue that will give $b$ modulo $u$. We can join these two residues in exactly $k-s-e$ steps. To see this, use only the two rising branches $f_-(z)=(4z-1)/3$ and $f_+(z)=(4z+1)/3$. If $A$ is the set reachable at a given time, the next set is
$$
f_-(A)\ \cup\ \bigl(f_-(A)+2/3\bigr)\pmod u.
$$
Translation by $2/3$ generates the additive group modulo $u$. The union thus has at least $|A|+1$ elements whenever $A$ is nonempty and proper. Starting from a singleton, all residues are reachable at every time at least $u-1$. For $u=1$ the statement is immediate.

Combine the three words. Its starting class modulo $3^k$ agrees with $a$ modulo $3^s$ by the prefix choice. The Chinese remainder theorem gives a positive representative $x\equiv a\pmod m$ realizing the whole word. All $x_j=x+j\,2^e u3^k$ for $j\ge0$ still realize it. Their final values agree with $b$ modulo $2^e u$ by the suffix and middle choices. Increasing $j$ by one increases the final value by $2^e uQ$, where $Q$ is the numerator of the full slope and is a power of 2. This increment is coprime to $3^s$, so infinitely many $j$ give the required final residue also modulo $3^s$. This proves the proposition.

**Consequence for the seed.** Taking $a=b=8\bmod m$ shows that every sufficiently long proposed return time passes the test that *some* integer in the seed's residue class can return to that class. Taking $a=11$, $b=8$ rules out separating the first iterate from the seed by a forward-invariant union of residue classes at any fixed modulus. Neither choice specifies $x=8$. The exact-return question for 8 remains open.

## Four leading binary digits along the original orbit

For $n>0$, write $s(n)=n/2^{\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor}\in[1,2)$ and $b(n)=\lfloor8s(n)\rfloor$. The eight values $8,\ldots,15$ represent the binary prefixes from 1000 through 1111, padding short integers on the right with zeros. A window of length $w$ includes its initial term and the next $w-1$ iterates.

**Theorem.** Outside the cycles $(1)$, $(2,3)$ and $(4,5,7,9,6)$, every 17-term window contains all eight prefixes, with exactly seven exceptional starting integers. In orbit order these are
$$
38,\quad 51,\quad 34,\quad 45,\quad 30,\quad 20,\quad 27.
$$
Each exceptional window misses exactly 1110. Every 24-term window outside the three cycles contains all eight prefixes, and the window of 23 terms beginning at 38 misses 1110. Thus 24 is sharp on this domain.

**The large-value argument.** Work on the positive reals modulo multiplication by powers of two, represented by $[1,2)$ with its endpoints identified on a logarithmic scale. Each exact step of the integer map has the form
$$
T(n)=\frac{2^a}{3}n(1+\delta),\qquad
(a,\delta)\in\{(1,0),(2,-1/(4n)),(2,1/(4n))\}.
$$
Both possible slopes therefore induce the same rotation, multiplication by $1/3$, before the rounding error. If all 17 terms are at least 1024, the cumulative factor from rounding at any of the 17 positions lies between $1/\rho$ and $\rho$, where $\rho=201/200$. Indeed $(1-1/4096)^{16}\ge1/\rho$ and $(1+1/4096)^{16}\le\rho$.

Normalize the 17 numbers $3^{-j}$, $0\le j\le16$, into $[1,2)$ by powers of two. In increasing order their largest successive ratio, including the gap from the last to twice the first, is $256/243$. This finite rational calculation is invariant under rotation of all the points by the initial significand. The bin for $h$ is $[h/8,(h+1)/8)$ and its endpoint ratio is at least $16/15$. Even after shrinking each bin at both ends by a factor $\rho$, its endpoint ratio exceeds the largest gap, since
$$
(256/243)\rho^2<16/15.
$$
An ideal point lies strictly inside each shrunken bin. Its true iterate lies in the original bin. This proves coverage for every window entirely above the cutoff.

**The finite part.** A remaining start belongs to
$$
\{T^{-j}(v):1\le v<1024,\ 0\le j\le16\}.
$$
The inverse is single-valued, so this list is exhaustive. It has 2,339 distinct members. The [exact checker](../checks/perm_1932_leading_bits.py) verifies the gap and both distortion inequalities with rational arithmetic, then computes the 17 prefixes at every candidate with integer arithmetic. It gives precisely the stated small cycles and seven further exceptions. Checking 24 terms at those seven starts and 23 at 38 proves sharpness. All other starts outside the three cycles already see every prefix in 17 terms.

**Consequence for 8.** The complete exceptional chain runs
$$
38\to51\to34\to45\to30\to20\to27\to18\to12\to8.
$$
Thus the forward orbit of 8 escapes if and only if **every** 17-term window on it contains 1110. A future exception would lead back to 8 along this chain. Conversely a periodic orbit through 8 must contain 38 because every predecessor is unique. Every 24-term window on the orbit of 8 contains all eight prefixes unconditionally. The step from 24 to 17 on this particular forward orbit remains open.

**Scope of the method.** For any fixed finite binary prefix beginning with 1, there is an effective finite procedure to classify all cycles that omit it. The ideal rotation is irrational because no positive power of 3 is a power of 2. Its finite initial segments therefore eventually have largest circular gap smaller than the prefix bin, and a strict inequality can be checked with rational arithmetic. Choose such a window and then a cutoff making the rounding errors fit its margin. The starts of windows missing the bin now belong to an explicit finite set of inverse iterates of small integers, as above. A cycle omitting the bin must lie entirely in this finite set, where its directed cycles can be enumerated. The four-bit theorem carries out this procedure for all eight bins at once.

## Deciding rational thresholds for leading-digit frequencies

Fix a binary prefix of length at least two starting with 1. Let $h$ be its integer value. As above, use the significand convention for short integers. Let $\beta_h(n)$ be the limiting fraction of terms on the forward orbit of $n$ with this prefix.

**Theorem.** This limit exists for every positive integer $n$. On an infinite orbit it equals $\gamma_h=\log_2((h+1)/h)$. On a finite orbit it equals the rational fraction obtained by counting once around the cycle. Given the prefix and any rational $r$, there is a terminating comparison of $\beta_h(n)$ with $r$, including an exact equality test. In fact one can list all the cycles whose frequency is equal to $r$ or on the opposite side of $r$ from $\gamma_h$.

**Proof and algorithm.** The prefix occupies an arc of length $\gamma_h$ on the circle of base-two logarithms. Its multiplicative width in significands is $(h+1)/h$. The number $\gamma_h$ is irrational. If $2^{p/q}=(h+1)/h$ with $p/q$ reduced, prime factorization forces $q=1$, whereas $1<(h+1)/h<2$. The side of a rational $r=p/q$ is therefore decidable by integer powers. It suffices to consider $0\le r\le1$.

When $\gamma_h>r$, use the prefix arc and put $t=r$. When $\gamma_h<r$, use its complement and put $t=1-r$. In either case the chosen arc has length strictly greater than $t$. Write $W$ for its multiplicative width. Choose $k$ such that for $u=\lfloor kt\rfloor+1$ the largest circular ratio $g$ from an ideal point to its $u$-th successor among the normalized $3^{-j}$, $0\le j<k$, satisfies $g<W$. This is an exact rational search, and doubling $k$ suffices.

To see termination, rotation by $\alpha=\log_2(4/3)$ is irrational. Its first $k$ points visit an arc with frequency tending to its length uniformly over the starting phase. For instance, a convergent $a/d$ with $|\alpha-a/d|<d^{-2}$ places each point of a $d$-term block within $1/d$ of a rotated equally spaced grid point. The count error for an arc is bounded by an absolute constant on each such block. Tiling a long segment by $d$-term blocks and then taking arbitrarily large $d$ proves uniformity. Apply this to a slightly shorter arc whose length still exceeds $t$.

Choose an integer $H$ large enough that
$$
g\left(\frac{4H+1}{4H-1}\right)^{k-1}<W.
$$
If all $k$ terms are at least $H$, the distortion bounds in the four-bit proof imply at least $u$ visits to the chosen arc. Indeed, shrinking the arc by the worst upper and lower cumulative distortions leaves room for $u$ ideal points strictly inside. On an infinite orbit the terms tend to infinity by injectivity, so every sufficiently late block satisfies this bound. Applying the argument to rational cuts approaching $\gamma_h$ on both sides proves the stated limiting frequency.

A cycle of length $L$ with chosen-arc frequency at most $t$ must contain a value below $H$. Among its $L$ cyclic windows at most $k(H-1)$ contain any such value, since each height occurs just once on a primitive cycle. Summing the window counts gives
$$
Lkt\ \ge\ (L-k(H-1))u,\qquad
L\ \le\ \frac{k(H-1)u}{u-kt}.
$$
This bounds both the least element and the period of every exceptional cycle. One can enumerate them, or just iterate a given seed to the period bound and test for return. In the absence of a return its frequency is strictly on the same side of $r$ as $\gamma_h$. The [exact implementation](../checks/perm_1932_prefix_frequency.py) includes both the bound and the seed comparison.

### A sharper calculation for the prefix 11

Put $b(n)=1$ if $n$ starts with 11, and $b(n)=0$ otherwise. Write $B_k(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} b(T^j n)$, $d(n)=\mathbf1_{3\mid n}$, $h_0(n)=\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ and $\theta(n)=\log_2 n-h_0(n)$. The exact bit-length identity is
$$
h_0(Tn)-h_0(n)=b(n)-d(n).
$$
Before rounding, the significand crosses the relevant power of two at $s(n)=3/2$. On a rising branch the output is an odd integer within $1/3$ of $4n/3$. Rounding cannot change its binary length: crossing a power of two from below would require rounding up to an even integer, and crossing from above would require a downward error greater than one. The case $n=1$ is direct. The falling branch is exact.

Let $e(n)=\log_2(Tn/(2^{2-d(n)}n/3))$. From the exact step formula,
$$
B_k(n)-k\alpha=\theta(n)-\theta(T^k n)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e(T^j n),
\qquad |e(n)|\ln2\le\frac1{4n-1}.
$$
On a primitive cycle of length $L$ and minimum $m$, the angular terms cancel and the $L$ heights are distinct. Its frequency $\beta$ therefore satisfies
$$
|\beta-\alpha|\ln2\le
\min\left(\frac1{4m-1},\,\frac{1+\ln L}{3L}\right).
$$
The second bound uses $4n-1\ge3n$ and the harmonic sum, as in the cycle-frequency estimate in section E1 of the project's orbit-of-8 handoff. The bit-length identity also shows that the number of terms starting with 11 equals the number of multiples of 3 on every cycle.

**Exact classification.** The only positive orbits with $\beta$ outside the open interval $(2/5,5/12)$ are the four cycles below. In particular, $2/5<\beta_3(8)<5/12$ whether or not 8 returns.

| Minimum | Period | Frequency of 11 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 0 |
| 2 | 2 | 1/2 |
| 4 | 5 | 2/5 |
| 44 | 12 | 5/12 |

For the exhaustive calculation, the natural-log distance of either endpoint from $\alpha$ (distance in frequency multiplied by $\ln2$) exceeds $1/900$. This follows from $\ln(256/243)/5$ and $\ln(3^{12}/2^{19})/12$ using $\ln v\ge(v-1)/v$. Any exceptional cycle thus has $m\le225$ and $L<300(1+\ln L)$, hence $L<4096$ by monotonicity of $(1+\ln L)/L$. The checker tests these possible minima and periods using integer iterates. Equality to the irrational $\alpha$ would settle escape for 8. The rational comparison algorithm does not settle that equality.

### A property of finite cycles equivalent to escape of 8

**Proposition.** The orbit of 8 escapes if and only if, on every finite positive cycle, the counts of prefix 11 in any two 65-term windows differ by at most one.

**Proof.** When the first 65 terms are all at least 2048, the bit-length identity gives $B_{65}=\lfloor\theta(n)+65\alpha+\sum e\rfloor$. The exact inequalities
$$
2^{26}<
\left[\tfrac43(1-1/8192)\right]^{65}<
\left[\tfrac43(1+1/8192)\right]^{65}<2^{27}
$$
force $B_{65}\in\{26,27\}$. The other possible starts are the 9,569 distinct integers $T^{-j}(v)$ with $1\le v<2048$, $0\le j<65$. The checker finds exactly five counts outside this pair.

| Start | 1 | 2 | 3 | 62 | 4055 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Count | 0 | 32 | 33 | 28 | 28 |

The first three starts belong to the fixed point and the 2-cycle. The 12-cycle through 44 contains 62 and its counts are 27 and 28. Each of those three cycles satisfies the asserted difference bound. The remaining exception satisfies $T^{53}(4055)=8$, while $T^{12}(8)=129$ and $B_{65}(129)=26$. A finite cycle through 8 therefore exhibits both 28 and 26. This is the only possible finite cycle with a difference of two at length 65. Every other finite cycle obeys the bound by the exhaustive list.

**Why the window counts alone cannot finish the proof.** Write $x_j=T^j(8)$ for signed $j$. The 64 bits beginning at $x_{-106}=2033$ agree with the 64 bits beginning at $x_{77}=16315$. The 183 intervening bits can therefore be repeated as a cyclic binary word. Its 65-term counts are 26 twice, 27 at 180 starts and 28 once. The endpoints 2033 and 16315 are different integers.

In fact there are arbitrarily long primitive cyclic binary words with this same unique count of 28, a count of 26, and a total number of ones within two of $\alpha$ times the length. To verify this, let $C_j$ be the cumulative number of ones in the common 64-bit prefix, $C_0=0$. The checker verifies
$$
\max_{0\le j\le64}2^{C_j}(3/4)^j
<2\min_{0\le j\le64}2^{C_j}(3/4)^j.
$$
The strict inequality gives an open interval of initial significands for the ideal rotation word, since the cumulative count at phase $t$ is $\lfloor t+j\alpha\rfloor$ for $0\le t<1$. Start at an interior phase and take an arbitrarily long segment ending back inside this interval by irrationality. Both ends begin with the common 64 bits, so one may insert the segment at the join of the 183-bit cyclic word. Every 65-window across a join is already a window of one of the two original sequences. The ideal segment has only counts 26 and 27, and its total count differs from its length times $\alpha$ by less than one. The original word has 76 ones and $|76-183\alpha|<1$. The unique window with 28 ensures primitivity. These words show explicitly where a proof of the proposition's balance condition must use the arithmetic of the integer heights.

## A rational cycle with the 183-bit word and its exact integer obstruction

Use the same branch formulas on rationals whose reduced denominators are coprime to 6. Read the residue of $a/b$ modulo 3 as $ab^{-1}\bmod 3$. Write $D(x)=2x/3$, $U_-(x)=(4x-1)/3$, and $U_+(x)=(4x+1)/3$. Their digits are 0, 1, and 2, respectively. This extension agrees with the original permutation on positive integers. Below, positivity of each constructed rational cycle is part of the conclusion.

**An explicit witness.** There is a positive rational cycle of primitive period 183 whose entire leading-bit word is precisely the 183-bit word of the preceding section, rotated to start at the window with count 28. Its minimum lies strictly between $8$ and $8+10^{-9}$. Its first 131 heights each exceed the corresponding integer on the orbit starting at 4055 by less than $10^{-5}$, and agree with that integer modulo 11. Its 65-term counts include both 28 and 26.

To reproduce the exact cycle, take the first 130 branch digits of the integer 4055. Append twelve zeros, the following 29 digits, and twelve more zeros:

    11121221121222122221222222212

For a word of length $L$ with $R$ rising steps, compose its affine maps to obtain $(2^{L+R}x+S)/3^L$. Its unique fixed point is $S/(3^L-2^{L+R})$. Every cyclic rotation has the same denominator before reduction, coprime to 6. The equations $3x_{i+1}=2^{a_i}x_i+s_i$ at successive rational positions force the prescribed residue of $x_i$ modulo 3. Thus the calculated fixed point genuinely follows the whole word. The [exact checker](../checks/perm_1932_rational_shadow.py) also reads the residues back independently, checks the leading bits, verifies distinctness of all 183 points, and compares the real heights by rational inequalities. The minimum occurs at position 53 and has a 286-bit reduced denominator.

**Sharp distinction on the integers.** In the specified phase, the infinite repetition of this word agrees with an integer orbit for exactly 235 initial bits at most, attained by 4055. Indeed its first two disjoint 65-term windows have counts 28 and 26. The preceding exhaustive classification allows only 62 or 4055 for the first window. The next window from 62 has count 27. This forces the integer start to be 4055. It agrees at positions 0 through 234. At position 235 its height is 6,237,833 and its bit is 0, whereas the periodic word has bit 1.

There is also an integer obstruction independent of the order of the bits. **No positive integer orbit has limiting frequency $76/183$ of prefix 11.** This is the exact frequency on the rational cycle. Applying the preceding rational-threshold theorem at $76/183>\alpha$ bounds the minimum of any exceptional integer cycle by $m<1395$ and its period by $L\le32768$. The checker enumerates all cycles within these two bounds. Their minima are $1,2,4,44$, with frequencies $0,1/2,2/5,5/12$. In particular the only positive integer cycles at or above this threshold are the ones through 2 and 44. Every infinite integer orbit has frequency $\alpha$.

### Approximation on any fixed finite part of the orbit of 8

**Theorem.** Let $B,F\ge0$ be integers, let $M\ge1$ be an integer, and let $\epsilon>0$. There exists a nonintegral positive rational periodic point $y$ whose whole cycle stays strictly above 8 and whose denominator is coprime to $6M$. Simultaneously for all $-B\le j\le F$,
$$
0<T^j(y)-T^j(8)<\epsilon,\qquad T^j(y)\equiv T^j(8)\pmod M.
$$
Congruence here uses inversion of the reduced denominator modulo $M$. The rational and integer paths follow the same branches on the prescribed segment. Taking the tolerance sufficiently small also preserves any prescribed finite number of leading bits, including at dyadic boundaries because the approximation is from above. In particular there are infinitely many such rational cycles with counts 28 and 26: keep the two 65-term windows, take $M=k!$, and let the tolerance tend to zero. Every prime divisor of the nontrivial reduced denominator then exceeds $k$, and the cycle minima tend to 8.

**Proof.** Factor $M=2^e3^v u$ with $\gcd(u,6)=1$. Extend the target segment backwards by $e$ steps and forwards by $v+1$ steps. Call the initial integer $z$, its forward branch word $W$, and its final integer $z'$. Every height of this segment is at least 8 because the integers below 8 belong to other cycles. We construct a cyclic branch word beginning with $W$ and with fixed point just above $z$.

For a contracting affine map use the seven consecutive branches $C=U_+^4 D^3$, written in chronological order. Their composite is
$$
x\longmapsto (2048x+1400)/2187.
$$
Its fixed point $p=1400/139$ exceeds 8. From any input at least $p$, a pass through these branches stays at least $p$: the first four steps increase and the last three decrease to an endpoint at least $p$. Repeating the word contracts to $p$.

Use a complete word in the following chronological order:
$$
W,\quad P,\quad C^r,\quad U_+^s,\quad D^t .
$$
Here $P$ consists of $\ell=\max(1,u-1)$ rising branches, with either sign allowed. From $z'\ge8$ even the smallest first rise reaches $31/3>p$, and both rising maps increase on this range.

Put $K=\varphi(u)$ for $u>1$, and $K=1$ for $u=1$. Take $r$ a multiple of $K$. Prescribe the residue of $s\bmod K$ so that the total number of rises is $0\bmod K$, and that of $t\bmod K$ so the total number of falls is $1\bmod K$. These prescriptions are independent of the signs in $P$ and of the chosen multiple $r$.

Fix a small $\eta>0$. We can choose positive $s,t$ in their prescribed classes so that the final two blocks send $p$ into $(z+\eta/8,z+\eta/4)$. Indeed their value is
$$
(p+1)(4/3)^s(2/3)^t-(2/3)^t .
$$
The ratio $\log(4/3)/\log(3/2)$ is irrational by unique factorization. Consequently $s\log(4/3)-t\log(3/2)$ is dense on the real line even within the two residue classes and with $s,t$ arbitrarily large: vary $s$ by $K$ and reduce modulo $K\log(3/2)$, then choose $t$ in its class. Target a smaller interior interval for the first term so that subtracting $(2/3)^t$ preserves the strict inequalities.

With $s,t$ fixed, choose $r$ sufficiently large in its class. Uniformly over the finitely many $P$, the full affine composite $H$ then satisfies $0<H(z)-z<\eta/2$ and has slope $0<\lambda<1/2$. This follows from contraction to $p$ and the choice of a strict interior interval. Its fixed point $x=z+(H(z)-z)/(1-\lambda)$ belongs to $(z,z+\eta)$.

It remains to choose the signs of $P$ for the congruence modulo $u$. When $u>1$, both rising maps are permutations modulo $u$, and their outputs differ by $2/3$, an additive generator. At each step a proper nonempty reachable set therefore grows by at least one, since the two images are translates of each other. After $\ell$ steps any residue is reachable from $z'$. The remaining suffix is an invertible affine map modulo $u$, so choose $P$ to give $H(z)\equiv z\bmod u$. For the total length $L$ and rise count $R$, the slope $\lambda=2^{L+R}/3^L$ is $2/3\bmod u$ by the prescribed counts. Hence $1-\lambda$ is a unit modulo $u$. It follows both that $x$ has denominator coprime to $u$ and that $x\equiv z\bmod u$. For $u=1$ these conclusions are automatic. The fixed-point calculation above gives denominators coprime to 6 at every position.

On $W$, each difference from the corresponding integer is $x-z$ times a fixed positive prefix slope. Choose $\eta$ in advance small enough that every difference is less than $\min(\epsilon,1)$. The path along $W$ thus stays strictly above 8 and is nonintegral there. The subsequent rising block and contracting loops stay above 8, and the last rises and falls never go below the smaller of their starting and ending heights, both above 8. Modulo $u$ the congruence propagates through $W$. Matching the preceding $e$ branches supplies a numerator factor $2^e$ in each difference on the requested segment. Matching the following $v$ branches forces divisibility by $3^v$ in each difference, since the difference $v$ steps later has denominator prime to 3. The padding ensures both assertions hold even at the endpoints. Taking the point corresponding to the integer 8 as $y$ proves the theorem.

For an explicit modular check, take the five-step word from 18, then four minus rises, 72 copies of $C$, 106 plus rises and 74 falls. The checker verifies a positive 693-cycle whose three successive values approximating $12,8,11$ each lie less than $1/2$ above their targets and coincide with them modulo 30.

**An integrality test involving the whole cycle.** If a rational periodic orbit stays at distance strictly less than $1/7$ from the integers at every position, it is an integer cycle. To see this, round each value $x_i$ to its nearest integer $n_i$. The integer $3n_{i+1}-2^{a_i}n_i-s_i$ has absolute value less than $(3+2^{a_i})/7\le1$, so it vanishes. Over a full cycle the errors $x_i-n_i$ are therefore multiplied by $2^{L+R}/3^L\ne1$ and must be zero. The approximation theorem controls any fixed segment, while this last criterion requires control around the entire period. Neither construction decides whether the integer 8 returns.
